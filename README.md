Chapter 1 | Definition and Structure of Gakubori

1.1 Definition of Gakubori

Gakubori is a system of expression in traditional Japanese tattooing (Irezumi) in which the background surrounding a primary subject—such as a person, animal, deity, Buddhist figure, or natural object—is composed through ink tonal variation, Bokashi shading, natural phenomena as motifs, and Mikiri borders.

Traditionally, elements such as clouds, wind, waves, vortices, rocks, flames, and light are combined to depict the space in which the primary subject exists.

Gakubori is one of the important features that distinguish traditional Japanese tattooing from various tattoo cultures around the world.

The gaku is not merely a background pattern. It visually emphasizes the subject while connecting imagery placed across multiple areas of the body, allowing those images to function as one continuous composition.

The primary functions of Gakubori are:

	1.	Subject emphasis: Visually separating the background from the primary subject through differences in light and dark.
	2.	Spatial construction: Depicting the environment—sky, land, water, and related spaces—in which the subject exists.
	3.	Creation of movement: Establishing directional flow and a sense of motion through wind, waves, vortices, and similar motifs.
	4.	Integration with body structure: Unifying the chest, shoulders, arms, back, and legs as a continuous pictorial field.
	5.	Definition of composition boundaries: Designing the boundary between tattooed areas and untattooed skin through Mikiri.
	6.	Narrative integration: Placing multiple pictorial subjects within a common space, season, or narrative world.
	7.	Control of tonal structure: Establishing a visual hierarchy among subjects, backgrounds, and bare skin.
	8.	Support for expansion: Allowing an initially partial tattoo to connect to a larger, potentially full-body composition.

Gakubori is both a decorative background and a structural component that makes it possible to organize the body as a unified composition.

Even a strong primary subject may lose legibility or coherence within the overall work if the background flow, tonal values, distance from the subject, or placement on the body is poorly resolved. Conversely, when the primary subject and gaku are successfully integrated, a continuous composition follows the body’s three-dimensional form in ways that differ from a flat image.

For these reasons, competence in Gakubori design can be regarded as an important part of the Japanese tattoo artist’s compositional ability.

Notes

Layers A–H are analytical categories used in this specification. They are not official historical terms shared by all traditional tattooing lineages.

Furthermore, some lineages use the term Keshōbori to encompass clouds, rocks, or waves; therefore, a definition that completely separates gaku from keshō is not universal.

1.3 Difference Between Gakubori and Nukibori

Nukibori — Tattooing without an enclosing background

Nukibori places the emphasis on the primary subject without adding a Gakubori background structure around it. The motif functions as a standalone composition, often leaving a substantial area of untattooed skin around it.

Gakubori — Tattooing with an integrated background

Gakubori adds natural motifs and a background to the primary subject, organizing a defined body area as a single pictorial composition. Multiple subjects can be placed within a continuous space and integrated into one cohesive work on the body.

Structural difference

In Nukibori, the outline of the subject itself often determines the overall silhouette of the tattoo. In Gakubori, a distinct outer structure is established around an area that contains both the subject and its background.

Accordingly, the available negative space and the conditions for positioning background elements differ between adding gaku after completing a Nukibori tattoo and designing the subject and gaku together from the outset.

1.4 Basic Definition of Contemporary Japanese Tattooing

At LOYAL TATTOO, Contemporary Japanese Tattooing (Gendai Wabori) refers to tattoo expression that takes the subjects, compositions, Gakubori, body placements, and natural motifs found in traditional Japanese Irezumi as its artistic foundation, then reconstructs them using contemporary techniques and structural design.

The essential point is not merely the use of traditional motifs. It is to understand the structural functions of those motifs and organize them into a composition that works on the human body.

This specification distinguishes historical records of traditional Japanese tattooing from the design standards defined for LOYAL TATTOO’s contemporary Japanese tattooing.



Chapter 2 | Historical Formation of Gakubori

2.1 The Edo Period and the Development of Tattoo Composition

The large-scale pictorial tattooing that led to contemporary traditional Japanese Irezumi is deeply connected to the ukiyo-e culture of the late Edo period.

Of particular importance is Utagawa Kuniyoshi’s series Tsūzoku Suikoden Gōketsu Hyakuhachinin no Hitori (One of the 108 Heroes of the Popular Water Margin).

Produced around 1827–1830, the series portrayed heroic figures bearing tattoos of dragons, peonies, Chinese guardian lions (Karajishi), and other motifs. It is believed to have strongly influenced Japanese decorative tattoo culture.

Warrior prints and characters from the Water Margin (Suikoden) became important visual references in the development of large-area pictorial tattoos on the human body.

However, it cannot be established that the tattoos depicted in ukiyo-e of that period followed precisely the same detailed rules as today’s Gakubori.

2.2 Overview of Historical Changes

|Period                      |Principal developments                                                                                         |
|----------------------------|---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|
|Early to middle Edo period  |Beauty marks (*irebokuro*), lettering, vows, and other forms of tattooing existed                              |
|Late Edo period             |Large pictorial tattoos developed under the influence of warrior prints, *Suikoden*, and related visual culture|
|Meiji to Taishō periods     |Tattoo techniques and designs continued to be transmitted despite prohibitive policies                         |
|Shōwa period                |Lineage-specific styles and background structures were inherited and further developed                         |
|Heisei period to the present|Machine tattooing and interaction with overseas methods expanded the available modes of expression             |

Gakubori as seen today rests on Edo-period ukiyo-e culture while also reflecting technical and stylistic developments across multiple generations of tattoo artists.

2.3 Clothing Culture and Traditional Body Placement

Body placement in traditional Japanese tattooing has also been related to clothing such as kimono and hanten jackets.

Historical photographs and artists’ records sometimes show arrangements with relatively small chest Taiko sections and broad areas of bare skin around the neck, wrists, and ankles compared with many contemporary works.

An aesthetic preference has also been passed down in which tattoos should not be extensively exposed through the collar, cuffs, or hems of a kimono.

By contrast, changes in clothing styles and everyday life have made it possible to see contemporary compositions with larger chest Taiko sections and border terminations closer to the wrists and ankles.

Alternative interpretation / historical aesthetic convention

A traditional explanation describes tattooing that extends broadly to the wrists and ankles as Dokatabori or Gaenbori, asserting that such arrangements could once be considered lacking in refinement when kimono were the usual clothing.

Under this view, a tattoo that is only slightly visible through openings in clothing was preferred to one that is extensively exposed.

These explanations nevertheless reflect particular periods and cultural judgments. They are not established as rules common to all regions and historical periods.

2.4 Traditional Full-Body Composition

Traditional Japanese Irezumi includes Sōshinbori, a full-body composition covering extensive parts of the body as one work.

One representative arrangement treats the back as the main pictorial field and extends outward to the shoulders, chest, arms, abdomen, and legs.

The subjects and gaku in each body area are connected to compose the entire body as a unified pictorial space.

Alternative interpretation / traditional order of production

Some accounts of older tattooing culture suggest that a major backpiece, rather than a smaller shoulder tattoo, was the standard starting point for a full-body work.

Contemporary practice, however, also commonly begins with a shoulder or arm composition that includes gaku. Starting with the back can therefore be described as a representative traditional approach, but not as the only possible order of production.



Chapter 3 | Classification of Natural Motifs in Gakubori

Natural motifs used in gaku can principally be organized into the following groups. This classification is based on compositional functions and is not intended as a complete list of officially recognized historical style names.

3.1 Wind Motifs — WIND

Motifs: Wind, wind lines, and flowing wind.

Basic structures

	●	Long, narrow, band-like lines.
	●	Curves flowing in a consistent direction.
	●	Repeated and overlapping sets of lines.
	●	Swirling or curling wind forms.
	●	Streamline structures formed through ink fields and untattooed skin.

Compositional function

Wind motifs give a stationary subject a direction of movement. For example, flowing background lines around a dragon can create motion within the surrounding space without substantially changing the subject itself.

However, the band-like forms generally called wind bars in English do not necessarily depict only wind. Technical explanations of traditional tattooing sometimes describe similar lines as representing waves, light, force, or momentum.

Key compositional considerations

	●	Relationship between wind direction and the subject’s movement.
	●	Line thickness and intervals of repetition.
	●	Continuity from shoulder to chest.
	●	Flow across the rotational surfaces of the arm.
	●	Connections to other natural motifs.
	●	Appearance of the streamlines from frontal and lateral viewpoints.

Straight-edged Gaku elements

Some Gakubori compositions contain repeated, relatively straight band-like lines. These are sometimes described internationally as wind bars.

However, band-like lines do not necessarily signify wind. They may also represent waves, light, or the broader movement of the picture.

Alternative explanation / terminology record

Some tattoo artists report that they have never heard a separate Japanese name for straight-lined gaku as distinguished from Iwa Mikiri (rock border) or Kumo Mikiri (cloud border).

Such accounts suggest that terminology for background structures is not completely standardized across Japan.

3.2 Cloud Motifs — CLOUD

Motifs: Clouds, curling clouds, cloud vortices.

Basic structures

	●	Rounded curves.
	●	Layered cloud outlines.
	●	Curling shapes.
	●	Variations in shading through Bokashi.
	●	Untattooed skin left between cloud formations.

Compositional function

Clouds establish atmospheric depth and relationships between foreground and background. They are used with subjects associated with the sky, such as dragons, phoenixes, and the wind and thunder gods (Fūjin and Raijin).

Clouds may surround a primary subject as background, or overlap that subject as foreground.

Technical considerations

Excessive cloud outlines can compete with the contours of the main subject. Drawing all clouds at the same size and tonal density can also turn the picture into a uniform repetition of motifs.

Spatial separation between front and back can be established by varying cloud size, overlap, and the depth of Bokashi shading.

Distinguishing clouds from Kumo Mikiri

Depicting clouds within the background differs, as a matter of classification, from using cloud shapes to define the outer border of the tattoo. The former is a natural motif; the latter is a Mikiri form.

3.3 Water Motifs — WATER

Motifs: Waves, flowing water, whirlpools, spray, bubbles, waterfalls.

Water motifs comprise several distinct forms.

|Motif        |Principal visual structure                 |Phenomenon represented            |
|-------------|-------------------------------------------|----------------------------------|
|Waves        |Continuous curves and wave crests          |Movement of a water surface       |
|Large waves  |Broad arcs and curling forms               |Strong water movement             |
|Whirlpools   |Spiral and rotational structures           |Rotational motion of water        |
|Flowing water|Long continuous curves                     |Water moving in one direction     |
|Waterfalls   |Vertical flowing lines                     |Falling water                     |
|Spray        |Small dots, short curves, and uninked areas|Water impact and dispersion       |
|Bubbles      |Repeated circles and ellipses              |Air within water or at its surface|

Representative subjects

Carp, goldfish, dragons underwater, figures at the water’s edge, and deities or legendary events associated with waterfalls.

For water motifs, the physical relationship between the subject and the water matters. A carp depicted ascending toward the Dragon Gate, for example, may be placed within an upward current or in opposition to a powerful flow.

Structural characteristics

In water-based gaku, flow direction is related to the posture and direction of movement of the subject.

A subject moving with the current, resisting the current, or positioned at the center of a vortex acquires a different compositional meaning even if the primary drawing is otherwise identical.

Spray and bubbles reinforce the motion of water while also creating light or uninked areas against dark ink fields.

3.4 Rock and Terrain Motifs — ROCK / EARTH

Motifs: Rocks, rocky formations, ground surfaces.

Basic structures

	●	Irregular polygonal forms.
	●	Angular contours.
	●	Lines suggesting strata and cracks.
	●	Large areas of light and dark.
	●	Connections to waves or waterfalls.

Compositional function

Rocks establish fixed terrain within the image, providing a sense of grounding and visual weight for the subject.

Their function contrasts with that of clouds and wind. Whereas clouds and wind represent movement, rocks provide a stable compositional foundation.

Representative applications

Fudō Myōō on a rocky seat, carp near rock formations, and waterfalls over rock faces.

Distinguishing rocks from Iwa Mikiri

Rocks may be drawn within the background, or their forms may be used to create the outer boundary of the tattoo. The latter is treated as Iwa Mikiri (rock-shaped border).

3.5 Flame Motifs — FLAME

Motifs: Flames, fire, flame halos.

Basic structures

	●	Curves with pointed ends.
	●	Repeated forms rising upward.
	●	Shapes extending outward from a central area.
	●	Strong light–dark separation between the flames and their surroundings.

Compositional function

Flames visualize combustion, heat, spiritual power, and related qualities. In some cases, such as the flames surrounding Fudō Myōō, they have specific iconographic meanings tied to the subject.

Flames may function as decorations belonging to the subject or as background natural phenomena. Depending on placement, they may be treated as part of the primary subject, keshō decoration, or gaku.

3.6 Thunder and Light Motifs — THUNDER / LIGHT

Motifs: Thunder, lightning, light.

Basic structures

	●	Sharply angled zigzag lines.
	●	Radiating curves and bands.
	●	Strong tonal contrast with surrounding dark areas.
	●	Combinations with clouds and wind.

Compositional function

These motifs express sudden energy, illumination, and changes in weather.

Whether lightning may be classified as a distinct Mikiri style, however, depends on the terminology used by individual tattoo artists.

3.7 Floral and Leaf Motifs — SEASONAL ORNAMENT

Motifs: Cherry blossoms, peonies, autumn leaves, chrysanthemums, lotus, morning glories, ginkgo, and others.

Flowers and leaves are frequently placed within gaku but may be classified more precisely as Keshōbori in certain systems of terminology.

|Motif                    |Principal function within the composition                         |
|-------------------------|------------------------------------------------------------------|
|Cherry blossoms          |Seasonal identification; falling petals; visualization of wind    |
|Peonies                  |Iconographic decoration associated with subjects such as Karajishi|
|Autumn leaves            |Autumn atmosphere; falling leaves; direction of wind              |
|Chrysanthemums           |Seasonality; adjustment of decorative density                     |
|Lotus                    |Relationship to Buddhist subjects                                 |
|Morning glories          |Seasonal atmosphere; specific narrative scenes                    |
|Ginkgo                   |Autumnal context; composition with falling leaves                 |
|Falling leaves and petals|Reinforcement of spatial depth and movement                       |

Important considerations

Flowers and leaves are not necessarily simple fillers for empty space.

Their placement can communicate season, the subject’s condition, and the direction of natural forces. In narrative tattoos, a particular plant may also identify the story being depicted.



Chapter 4 | Classification of Mikiri Border Styles

4.1 Definition of Mikiri

Mikiri refers to the boundary between an area containing Gakubori and the area of skin left untattooed.

In traditional Japanese tattooing, however, Mikiri does not always refer only to the boundary line itself.

Adding gaku to a Nukibori tattoo may be called adding Mikiri (Mikiri o ireru or Mikiri o tsukeru), and the term may sometimes be used more broadly to include the entire background.

Names such as Iwa Mikiri and Kumo Mikiri combine boundary form with a natural motif.

Mikiri therefore has several usages:

	1.	The boundary between the gaku and bare skin.
	2.	The contour shape used for the boundary.
	3.	A shading technique applied at the boundary.
	4.	A conventional expression referring to the gaku as a whole.

4.2 Origin of the Term Mikiri

The etymology of Mikiri is not conclusively established.

In architecture, mikiri denotes elements used to finish the boundary between different materials or finishes. In picture framing, related terminology is also used for elements defining a pictorial edge.

These uses share with tattoo Mikiri the function of defining a boundary. No historical evidence has been confirmed, however, that establishes direct derivation of the tattoo term from architectural or picture-framing terminology.

Alternative account / oral tradition

Another theory connects adding gaku with severing ties to conventional civilian society, interpreting mikiri through the expression katagi no sekai ni mikiri o tsukeru—roughly, “to leave the respectable civilian world behind.”

This may be a later folk explanation; it is not established as the term’s etymology.

4.3 Natural Motifs Represented by Mikiri

Mikiri can depict natural phenomena as well as define a work’s outer edge.

Rock forms may be called Iwa Mikiri, and cloud forms Kumo Mikiri. Band-shaped lines may suggest wind, waves, light, force, or momentum.

A central point is that the shape of Mikiri and the natural phenomenon it represents do not always correspond on a one-to-one basis.

4.5 Botan Mikiri — BOTAN MIKIRI

A border made from repeated curves resembling semicircles or fans.

Characteristics

	●	Repeated curves.
	●	Can create a relatively substantial sense of mass.
	●	Used on the chest, shoulders, arms, legs, and other areas.
	●	Rounded, continuous outer profile.
	●	Rhythmic variation in the border’s curved outline.

The word Botan (peony) names the form of the border; it does not mean that a peony flower must be tattooed as a subject.

Alternative interpretation / practical usage

Some traditional tattoo artists adopt Botan Mikiri as their standard style when no special request has been made. Some also regard its border as having an elegant appearance.

These practices reflect individual styles and are not a universal standard across all lineages.

4.6 Butsugiri — BUTSUGIRI / BUKKIRI

A style in which the Mikiri terminates along a straight edge.

Characteristics

	●	A clear border.
	●	Often used at sleeve ends and leg terminations.
	●	Contrasts with the curves making up much of the background.
	●	The position and shape of the termination affect the work as a whole.

Design characteristics

When a straight termination wraps around a cylindrical part of the body, its apparent levelness and visual alignment become important.

It is a representative method for sleeve cuffs and leg endings but can also be applied elsewhere.

Alternative interpretation / aesthetic assessment

Butsugiri is sometimes described as appearing sharper than Botan Mikiri. The resulting impression, however, changes with border width, ink density, and the surrounding gaku structure.

4.7 Akebono Mikiri — AKEBONO MIKIRI

A style in which ink density changes progressively so that the boundary fades into untattooed skin.

Characteristics

	●	May have no distinctly outlined outer edge.
	●	Ink tonal variation is a principal structural element.
	●	The boundary changes smoothly.

Technical distinction

Akebono Mikiri refers to the treatment of the boundary, whereas Akebono Bokashi refers to the technique of creating a tonal transition. They are related but not entirely synonymous.

4.8 Matsuba Mikiri — MATSUBA MIKIRI

A style using repeated short lines to make a border resembling pine needles.

Characteristics

	●	Repeated lines.
	●	Needle-like or comb-like termination.
	●	A structure combining lines and exposed skin.
	●	Mentioned in records and tattoo artists’ accounts of older styles.

It is reported to be encountered less often in ordinary contemporary work than Botan Mikiri or Butsugiri.

4.9 Jari Mikiri — JARI MIKIRI

A style in which the border is formed from dots of ink.

Characteristics

	●	Groups of dots.
	●	Tonal variation controlled through dot density.
	●	Granular transitions to bare skin.
	●	Technical relationship to Jari Bokashi.

Its distinction from Akebono Mikiri lies in the difference between a continuous tonal fade and a transition made using visible collections of dots.

4.10 Other Mikiri Names

Iwa Mikiri — Rock border

A border using rock shapes and reflecting a rocky structure in the outer edge.

Kumo Mikiri — Cloud border

A border using cloud forms, with rounded outlines or recurring cloud-like shapes.

Juzu Mikiri — Prayer-bead border

A termination style incorporating the motif of juzu (Buddhist prayer beads) at the edge of a tattoo. Examples near the wrist can be found.

Juzu Mikiri differs in its classificatory nature from Butsugiri and Botan Mikiri: it uses a specific decorative object to define the termination.

4.11 Historical Usage

Botan Mikiri and Butsugiri are representative forms still visible in contemporary traditional Japanese tattoos.

Some tattoo artists state that Akebono Mikiri, Matsuba Mikiri, and Jari Mikiri can be observed in older ukiyo-e or related imagery but are rarely used today.

No comprehensive statistics have been confirmed that quantify their frequency in contemporary practice.

4.12 Left–Right Consistency of Mikiri

In Munewari compositions and on both chest Taiko sections, the left and right Mikiri form a shared, potentially symmetrical structure.

The following elements are especially important:

	●	Width of lines on the left and right.
	●	Ink density.
	●	Intervals between repeated curves.
	●	Placement relative to the chest centerline.
	●	Flow from shoulder to chest.
	●	End positions of the borders.

The left and right compositions need not be identical, but unintentional differences in line width and tonal balance can affect the coherence of the overall work.

4.13 Conclusion: Classification of Mikiri

Mikiri cannot be fully understood as a single flat list of styles.

Axis 1: Contour shape

	●	Curved.
	●	Straight.
	●	Rock-like.
	●	Cloud-like.

Axis 2: Tonal termination

	●	Clearly defined ending.
	●	Akebono-style fade.
	●	Dotted transition.
	●	Linear transition.

Axis 3: Termination using decorative objects

	●	Borders incorporating juzu or similar objects.

Separating these three axes allows styles of different structural character to be described without conflating them.



Chapter 5 | System of Bokashi Shading Techniques

The final appearance of gaku depends not only on its outlines but also on the density and tonal gradation of ink.

5.1 Representative Bokashi Categories

|Technique                     |Description                                        |Principal use                       |
|------------------------------|---------------------------------------------------|------------------------------------|
|Standard Bokashi              |Gradual changes in ink tone                        |Basic three-dimensional rendering   |
|*Akebono Bokashi*             |Smooth fade toward the boundary                    |Soft tonal transitions              |
|*Jari Bokashi*                |Gradations made through dot density                |Granular shading                    |
|*Kaze Bokashi* (wind shading) |Shading following a wind-like flow                 |Streamlined background elements     |
|*Kumo Bokashi* (cloud shading)|Shading matched to cloud volume                    |Layered cloud structures            |
|*Nami Bokashi* (wave shading) |Shading matched to waves and currents              |Depth and force of moving water     |
|*Mizu Bokashi* (water shading)|Tonal treatment suggesting transparency and bubbles|Underwater and water-surface imagery|
|*Tsubushi* (solid filling)    |Uniformly dense ink application                    |Dark areas and solid black fields   |

The names and specific applications of Bokashi techniques vary by lineage and tattoo artist.

5.2 Three Functions of Bokashi

Luminance separation

Varying the brightness of a subject and its gaku makes it possible to distinguish foreground from background.

If the subject’s outline and the background are close in luminance, the boundary between them may become unclear, weakening the visual hierarchy within the picture.

Bokashi is therefore an important factor in subject legibility because it establishes the intended difference between the subject and background.

Representation of volume

Gradation within rocks, waves, clouds, and similar forms allows them to read as objects with mass rather than mere outlined shapes.

Even where natural motifs share the same outer contour, the distribution of ink density within them changes the apparent thickness, overlap, and depth of the forms.

For example, darkening one side of a rock while leaving the other lighter can make it appear as three-dimensional terrain.

For clouds and waves, the combination of curved contours and the direction of shading similarly generates the sense of volume appropriate to the phenomenon depicted.

Spatial connection

Controlling tonal values between different natural motifs can make them read as one continuous pictorial space.

Wind, clouds, waves, and rocks have different shapes but may connect inside the same gaku. If their gradations or the directional continuity of dark fields are poorly resolved, the motifs may appear to be independent patterns.

Bokashi helps integrate these different forms into a shared space.

5.3 Relationship Between Black Fields and Bare Skin

Both inked black areas and skin deliberately left untattooed form part of a Gakubori composition.

Important design variables include:

	●	Proportion of the area occupied by black fields.
	●	Proportion occupied by bare skin.
	●	Maximum tonal contrast.
	●	Continuity and direction of ink fields.
	●	Negative space surrounding the main subject.
	●	Tonal density near the Mikiri.
	●	Overall distribution and balance of lights and darks.

No universally applicable numerical standard for these variables has been confirmed.

Nevertheless, the distribution of black fields and bare skin influences the work’s apparent visual weight, the readability of subjects, and the flow of the background.

In large-scale Gakubori, evenly distributing black across the image is not necessarily optimal. Dark and light areas must be planned in relation to the subject’s position, the form of the natural motifs, and the curvature of the body.

5.4 Asenuki — ASENUKI

Asenuki is a term for an area of bare skin intentionally left uncolored and uninked inside the contour of a subject or another outlined component.

It may appear in tattoos on the back, arms, legs, and other body regions.

Asenuki is not simply an unfinished or uncolored area: it functions as negative space that forms part of the composition’s structure.

Its principal functions include:

	●	Preserving tonal contrast within the primary subject.
	●	Visually separating contour lines from colored areas.
	●	Reinforcing the subject’s apparent three-dimensionality.
	●	Regulating the density of black across the composition.
	●	Establishing the tonal relationship between subject and gaku.

Asenuki and cover-up tattoos

When an existing tattoo is covered with another design, the planned Asenuki areas may present design constraints.

If existing pigment occupies skin intended to remain bare in the new design, the intended negative space cannot be reproduced without alteration.

Consequently, the same tonal design cannot always be recreated when working over an existing tattoo as when starting on previously untattooed skin.

Alternative account / etymology of Asenuki

One traditional explanation claims that Asenuki originated as a deliberate way to leave bare skin and thereby show that a decorative tattoo was not intended to conceal an Edo-period penal tattoo (bokkei).

Reliable historical evidence confirming this explanation has not been identified, and it cannot be stated as an established etymology.

5.5 Structural Classification of Negative Space

This specification distinguishes three types of space formed by bare skin.

|Classification                 |Location                             |Principal function                                     |
|-------------------------------|-------------------------------------|-------------------------------------------------------|
|Subject-internal negative space|Within the primary subject’s contours|Producing tonal structure inside the subject           |
|Gaku-internal negative space   |Within the background                |Forming clouds, wind, waves, and other motifs          |
|Exterior negative space        |Outside the Mikiri                   |Separating the tattooed work from untattooed body areas|

All three comprise the same uninked skin, but they serve different roles within the work.

Subject-internal negative space

Areas left inside a subject, such as the skin of a depicted person, portions of an animal, or parts of clothing. They contribute to the form and tonal structure of the subject itself.

Gaku-internal negative space

Areas that form background features, such as cloud contours, wave spray, and the bands of wind-like elements. They are essential to defining natural motifs.

Exterior negative space

Untattooed skin beyond the Mikiri, separating the tattoo composition from surrounding body areas.

5.6 Bokashi and Structural Design in Contemporary Japanese Tattooing

LOYAL TATTOO treats Bokashi not merely as an auxiliary coloring technique but as a design component used to build a three-dimensional pictorial field.

Its structure may be evaluated through the following criteria:

|Evaluation criterion      |Question or consideration                                                          |
|--------------------------|-----------------------------------------------------------------------------------|
|Tonal separation          |Can the primary subject be distinguished from the gaku by differences in luminance?|
|Gradation continuity      |Do tonal changes follow the intended composition?                                  |
|Spatial depth             |Are foreground, middle ground, and background relationships expressed?             |
|Black-field allocation    |Are dark ink fields positioned structurally within the composition?                |
|Negative-space allocation |Does bare skin contribute to the representation of form and depth?                 |
|Continuity across the body|Are tonal relationships preserved across the body’s curved surfaces?               |



Chapter 6 | Body Placement Formats

Gakubori is classified not only according to its natural motifs but also according to the body area covered by a completed composition.

6.1 Arm Formats — ARM COMPOSITION

In traditional Japanese tattooing, work extending from the shoulder down the arm may be described with terms such as kata (shoulder), ude (arm), or kaina (arm).

Connecting the arm gaku to the chest Taiko or Hikae is a representative traditional arrangement.

|Format                                                                       |General extent                        |Notes                                              |
|-----------------------------------------------------------------------------|--------------------------------------|---------------------------------------------------|
|*Gobu-sode* (half sleeve; literally five-tenths sleeve)                      |Shoulder to around the elbow          |Length standards vary by artist                    |
|*Shichibu-sode* (three-quarter sleeve; literally seven-tenths sleeve)        |Below the elbow onto the forearm      |Does not mean exactly 70%                          |
|*Hachibu-sode* (extended three-quarter sleeve; literally eight-tenths sleeve)|Farther down the forearm than Shichibu|Names and termination positions vary among lineages|
|*Kyūbu-sode* (near-full sleeve; literally nine-tenths sleeve)                |Close to the wrist                    |Sometimes called a long sleeve                     |
|*Nagasode* (full sleeve; wrist-length Japanese tattoo sleeve)                |To the vicinity of the wrist          |Defined by a Mikiri at the sleeve end              |

Traditional compositions emphasize continuity between the arm and chest Hikae. However, contemporary tattooing also includes arm-only compositions with gaku and without a chest section; these should not automatically be identified as identical to traditional body-placement forms.

6.2 Gobu-sode — Half Sleeve (Literally Five-Tenths Sleeve)

A sleeve covering the shoulder and upper arm to approximately the elbow.

One traditional practical guideline places the border about two finger-widths above the elbow. This is not a strict nationwide measurement standard.

The actual boundary depends on the subject’s size, upper-arm length, muscular shape, and relationship with the chest Taiko.

Structural characteristics

	●	A subject is positioned across the shoulder and upper arm.
	●	The composition may connect to the chest Taiko.
	●	The gaku terminates above the elbow.
	●	The shape of the sleeve’s ending changes the overall visual impression.

Relationship to clothing

A Gobu-sode (half sleeve) may be partly visible under a short-sleeved T-shirt. Because exposure varies with clothing sleeve length, the relationship between the body’s Mikiri position and the clothing is also a design condition.

Alternative interpretation / sleeve-length aesthetics

Some traditional tattoo artists consider an extremely short Gobu-sode (half sleeve) visually unbalanced as a traditional gaku composition.

Some also argue that Sanbu-sode (three-tenths sleeve) is not an official traditional term. These opinions reflect the terminology used by individual artists or lineages; they do not mean that a shorter sleeve is technically impossible.

6.3 Shichibu-sode — Three-Quarter Sleeve (Literally Seven-Tenths Sleeve)

A sleeve extending below the elbow onto the forearm.

The Mikiri is generally positioned around the middle of the area between the elbow and wrist. The word Shichibu does not mean that the total arm has been mathematically divided at precisely 70 percent.

Structural characteristics

	●	Integrates upper arm and forearm into a single pictorial field.
	●	Extends the gaku across the elbow’s curved surface.
	●	Provides more area for subjects and natural motifs.
	●	Leaves untattooed skin near the wrist.

On Hachibu sleeves

A format extending somewhat farther than Shichibu-sode (three-quarter sleeve) may be called Hachibu-sode (extended three-quarter sleeve).

Alternative account / meaning of Hachibu

Some tattoo artists are said to favor the term Hachibu because the number eight (hachi) is associated with auspicious expansion in Japanese culture. The chosen name nevertheless does not necessarily correspond to an exact sleeve length.

6.4 Kyūbu-sode — Near-Full Sleeve (Literally Nine-Tenths Sleeve)

A format extending the gaku close to the wrist.

Depending on lineage and usage, it may also be described as a Nagasode (full sleeve, extending to the wrist), although Kyūbu-sode and Nagasode should not automatically be treated as exact synonyms.

Structural characteristics

	●	A long, continuous composition from shoulder to the distal forearm.
	●	Incorporates the elbow and rotational surfaces of the forearm.
	●	Terminates at a sleeve-edge Mikiri.
	●	Can join the chest Taiko to integrate a broad surface of the body.

Relationship to clothing

Where tattooing reaches the bony prominences near the wrist, portions of the tattoo may become visible even under long sleeves when the arms extend or move.

The observable extent of a tattoo thus changes with movement; Mikiri placement should not be considered only in a stationary posture.

6.5 Chest Formats — CHEST COMPOSITION

Hikae (控え)

A section extending from the shoulder across the chest.

Taiko (太鼓)

A rounded projection of gaku onto the chest.

Shallow Hikae

A form projecting relatively little onto the chest.

Deep Hikae

A form extending farther toward the center of the chest.

Eguri (えぐり)

A curved structure preserving bare skin around the neck or collar area.

6.6 Relationship Between Taiko and Hikae

In practice, Taiko and Hikae are used to describe the arrangement of gaku that extends from the shoulder onto the chest. Their precise scope varies among tattoo artists.

For the purposes of this specification, Hikae denotes the overall structure extending onto the chest, while Taiko denotes its rounded projection.

6.7 Size of the Chest Taiko

The area occupied by a Taiko depends principally on:

	●	Width of the rib cage.
	●	Shoulder width.
	●	Shape of the pectoral muscles.
	●	Position of the nipples.
	●	Size of the main subject.
	●	Flow of the Mikiri from shoulder to chest.
	●	Relationship to the neckline of clothing.

Because anatomy differs between individuals, identical Taiko dimensions cannot be applied to everyone.

Alternative interpretation / whether Taiko is required

Some traditional tattoo artists maintain that shoulder gaku should include a chest Taiko and describe arm-only compositions without Taiko as closer to American tattooing than traditional Japanese Irezumi.

This specification recognizes the chest Taiko as a major traditional compositional component while distinguishing the existence of contemporary arm-only gaku arrangements without Taiko.

It does not categorically state that work without Taiko cannot be considered traditional Japanese tattooing.

6.8 Back Formats — BACK COMPOSITION

Segaku / Sewari (背額・背割り)

A background composition that uses the back as its main pictorial field and may extend across the shoulders, back, buttocks, and thighs.

Kame no Kō / Kōra (亀の甲・甲羅)

Terms associated with formats covering a broad area of the back, evoking a turtle shell.

Extending the lower end of a backpiece to the thighs is regarded as a representative traditional large-scale arrangement; however, the terminology and actual extent vary among lineages.

6.9 Structure of Back Gaku

The back has one of the largest continuous surfaces of the body and is suitable for large primary subjects.

The curved surfaces of the shoulder blades, spine, lower back, and buttocks can be used to develop several visual elements as one pictorial field.

Traditional back gaku may include a vertical Mikiri near the sides of the torso, separating the back’s composition from the chest or abdominal region.

6.10 Role of the Back in Full-Body Composition

The back can serve as the primary reference surface when designing a full-body tattoo.

By positioning the main narrative subject on the back and developing related figures or natural motifs across the chest, arms, abdomen, and legs, the entire body can be composed as one narrative system.

Alternative account / traditional sequence of production

Some older Japanese tattooing traditions are said to have begun with a major backpiece rather than the shoulders. Another view regards beginning on the back as the most rational means of unifying the full-body composition.

Starting from the shoulders is also well established in contemporary work. Beginning with the back is therefore not the sole formally acceptable production sequence.

6.11 Front-Torso Formats — FRONT TORSO

Munewari (胸割り)

A format leaving a vertical strip of bare skin along the central axis from the chest toward the abdomen.

Guidelines for the gap include the width of a fist or four fingers, but its actual width changes with body size and composition.

Donburi / Sōzume (ドンブリ・総詰め型)

A composition tattooing across the center of the chest and abdomen without leaving the central strip open.

The term Donburi may also refer to an extensive or nearly full-body tattoo rather than only the front-torso configuration.

6.12 Munewari — MUNEWARI

A format preserving a vertical area of untattooed skin along the centerline from chest toward abdomen. A continuous negative-space strip separates the tattooed areas on the left and right.

Structural characteristics

	●	Negative space aligned with the body’s median line.
	●	Parallel Mikiri on the left and right.
	●	Continuity from chest to abdomen.
	●	Integration of subjects and backgrounds on both sides.
	●	Connections between the front torso and the back or arms.

Width of the Munewari gap

Traditional practical guidelines sometimes describe four finger-widths or one fist-width. The correct width, however, depends on the wearer’s dimensions and the shape of the rib cage; a single measurement cannot apply to every body.

Alternative account / historical origin

One explanation suggests that Munewari developed in imitation of the opening of a hanten jacket. Another tradition holds that the central gap was wider when kimono were more commonly worn.

The precise dates and origins of these arrangements are not fully established.

6.13 Donburi — DONBURI

A front-torso arrangement tattooed across the middle of the chest and abdomen, without the vertical strip of bare skin found in Munewari.

The entire front of the torso may thereby function as one large pictorial field.

Structural characteristics

	●	The front torso is treated as a continuous picture.
	●	No central negative-space strip corresponding to Munewari is retained.
	●	Large subjects can be placed on the abdomen.
	●	Continuity with both chest sections, the shoulders, and the back is important.

Alternative interpretation / scope of the term Donburi

One explanation says that Donburi originally referred to full-body tattooing extending to the wrists and ankles. In practical usage, however, a composition may be called Donburi whenever the front torso is filled, even if the tattoo does not reach the wrists or ankles.

This specification acknowledges the latter usage while also recording the application of Donburi to full-body arrangements.

6.14 Placement of Subjects on the Abdomen

Like the back, the abdomen can accommodate a large primary subject.

However, when Taiko sections on both shoulders and the chest have already been completed, the space available for an independent abdominal subject may be restricted.

When the abdomen is intended as a large pictorial field, one approach is to design the chest Taiko and abdominal field together. This is also related to the traditional approach of developing full-body compositions outward from the back.

6.15 Neck Opening — NECK OPENING

A format preserving an area of bare skin around the neck.

|Format    |Shape   |Structural characteristic                                       |
|----------|--------|----------------------------------------------------------------|
|Round neck|Arc-like|Relatively even negative space around the neck                  |
|V-neck    |V-shaped|Angular negative space projecting toward the center of the chest|
|U-neck    |U-shaped|Broad, curved negative space extending onto the chest           |

The depth and extent of the neck opening depend on neck, shoulder, and chest anatomy, subject size, and clothing.

The opening is not merely an untattooed area: it also establishes the upper termination of the gaku.

6.16 Leg Formats — LEG COMPOSITION

In traditional full-body compositions, leg gaku is usually conceived in relation to the torso and back.

|Format                                                            |General extent                                               |
|------------------------------------------------------------------|-------------------------------------------------------------|
|Taiko-type                                                        |Rounded continuation of torso gaku onto the back of the thigh|
|*Hanzubon* (shorts-style leg tattoo)                              |To around the area above the knee                            |
|*Shichibu / Hachibu* (seven-tenths / eight-tenths leg formats)    |From below the knee to a point above the ankle               |
|*Kyūbu / Nagazubon* (near-ankle or long-trousers-style leg tattoo)|Close to the ankle                                           |
|*Tabi-type*                                                       |Extended onto the top of the foot                            |

6.17 Leg Taiko Format

A format that extends the Mikiri from the chest and abdomen downward, rounding it as it connects to the back of the thigh.

The same term used for Taiko on the shoulder or chest may be used, although the placement and structure differ.

Alternative interpretation / applicability

Some tattoo artists associate the leg Taiko format with Munewari and report having seen no examples of its use with Donburi.

No evidence has been confirmed establishing that its use with Donburi is technically impossible.

6.18 Hanzubon — Shorts-Style Leg Tattoo

A format ending the gaku partway down the thigh, generally above the knee. No single standardized termination height exists.

Structural characteristics

	●	Subjects are concentrated on the thigh.
	●	Connections with the waist and buttocks are possible.
	●	A large area of bare skin remains below the knee.
	●	The Mikiri produces a visual termination resembling the hem of shorts.

The shorts-type ending is also found in some Sewari compositions.

6.19 Shichibu Leg Format — Seven-Tenths Leg Coverage

A format extending below the knee to roughly the middle of the lower leg.

As with the seven-tenths arm sleeve, the name does not mathematically designate exactly 70 percent of the total length. The Mikiri generally falls between the knee and the ankle bone.

6.20 Nagazubon / Kyūbu Leg Format — Long-Trousers Style / Nine-Tenths Coverage

A format extending the gaku toward the ankles, unifying the thigh, knee, and lower leg into a continuous pictorial field.

Some arrangements extend close to the bony prominences of the ankles.

Alternative interpretation / border near the ankle

Some tattoo artists explain that pigmentation near the ankle bones can appear to fade over time and therefore recommend a slightly higher border to preserve the appearance of the outline.

Pigment retention depends on several factors, however, including skin condition, friction, and elapsed time; it cannot be determined from location alone.

6.21 Tabi Format

A format extending leg gaku below the ankle and onto the top of the foot. Its outline resembles the shape created by wearing traditional Japanese tabi footwear, which gives rise to the name.

6.22 Independent Leg Gakubori

In traditional full-body composition, leg gaku is often connected with the back, chest, and abdomen. Contemporary tattooing also includes designs where gaku is applied to a leg alone.

Alternative interpretation / traditional aesthetic preference

Some traditional tattoo artists express reservations about completing leg gaku as an independent work. This perspective arises from production systems that treat leg backgrounds as parts of a full-body composition.

It does not mean that independent leg gaku is incapable of functioning as a contemporary tattoo composition.

Within LOYAL TATTOO’s contemporary Japanese tattooing, an independent leg tattoo may be treated as a valid standalone design, provided that its difference from traditional full-body composition is made explicit.



Chapter 7 | Comparison of Kantō and Kansai Styles

Regional differences in Japanese traditional tattooing are sometimes described as Kantō-bori and Kansai-bori.

7.1 Regional Compositional Tendencies

|Comparison                      |Tendencies associated with Kantō      |Tendencies associated with Kansai   |
|--------------------------------|--------------------------------------|------------------------------------|
|Width of gaku and Mikiri        |Relatively narrow                     |Relatively wide                     |
|Depth of Hikae                  |Often contained above the nipple      |Often extends to or below the nipple|
|Area occupied on the chest      |Relatively small                      |Relatively large                    |
|Overall compositional impression|Composition within a more limited area|Composition using a broader area    |

These descriptions indicate reported historical tendencies, not a regional classification applicable to every contemporary artist.

In practice, the artist’s training lineage, personal style, and the client’s physique all affect the format.

7.2 Sleeve Length and Regional Conventions

Alternative account / historical regional classification

One account associates Gobu-sode (half sleeves; literally five-tenths sleeves) whose chest Taiko ended above the nipples with Kantō-bori, and Shichibu-sode (three-quarter sleeves; literally seven-tenths sleeves) with Taiko extending to the nipple area with Kansai-bori.

Today, however, regional styles cannot reliably be distinguished by sleeve length and Taiko depth alone.

Different lineages and techniques coexist within the same regions, and body placement is selected for each individual work.

7.3 Gaku Line Width and Regional Differences

One view holds that finer gaku lines were more common in Kantō and areas farther east, while broader lines were more common in Kansai and farther west.

Styles vary within each region, however, and line width alone cannot conclusively identify where a tattoo was produced or the artist’s training lineage.

7.4 Regional Styles in Contemporary Japanese Tattooing

This specification treats Kantō and Kansai formats as historical reference categories.

LOYAL TATTOO’s contemporary Japanese tattooing does not make either regional style the sole rule. Instead, the overall work is established through its subjects, body shape, Mikiri, tonal structure, and compositional design.



Chapter 8 | Relationships Between Primary Subjects and Gaku

The relationship between subject and background is an important design condition in traditional Japanese tattooing.

8.1 Representative Combinations

|Dragon                           |Clouds, wind, thunder, rain, water               |Relationships with weather and water          |

|Tiger                            |Rocks, wind, terrain, plants                     |Terrestrial setting and movement              |
|Warriors and human figures       |Rocks, waves, wind, clouds, etc.                 |Establishing a narrative scene                |
|Goldfish                         |Water, currents, bubbles                         |Representation of underwater space            |

These are representative combinations, not an exclusive list of permitted pairings.

The appropriate background for a person, deity, or legendary being varies according to the specific scene depicted.

8.2 Principle of Vertical Spatial Ordering

Some traditional tattooing style explanations describe a compositional order proceeding upward through:

Water → Ground → Sky → Heavens

This method provides a basic way to integrate multiple natural phenomena into a single pictorial field.

It does not mean that all four layers must appear in every tattoo.

8.3 Seasonal Consistency

Combining cherry blossoms, peonies, chrysanthemums, autumn leaves, lotus, morning glories, ginkgo, and other plants raises questions about seasonal interpretation.

In a work depicting a single scene, keeping the season consistent can preserve the coherence of its natural setting.

Works depicting multiple legends or scenes simultaneously need not be limited to one season.

Alternative interpretation / traditional seasonal unity

A convention has been described in which the flowers across the entire body need not be the same species, but seasonality is kept consistent within each distinct body region—for example, within the back or within the shoulder composition.

This approach treats different body regions as independent scenes while maintaining coherence within each scene.

8.4 Relationships Between Subjects and Specific Plants

Certain combinations of subjects and plants recur in traditional tattoo designs.

|Subject                          |Related plants                                    |Principal relationship                                     |
|---------------------------------|--------------------------------------------------|-----------------------------------------------------------|
|Karajishi                        |Peony                                             |*Karajishi Botan*                                          |
|Tiger                            |Bamboo, bamboo grass, peony                       |Traditional animal-and-plant combinations                  |
|Dragon                           |Chrysanthemum, cherry blossom, autumn leaves, etc.|Combinations chosen according to subject and season        |
|Danshichi Kurobei                |Morning glory                                     |Connection to a particular narrative scene                 |
|Spirit of the Komachi Cherry Tree|Cherry blossoms                                   |Plant integral to the subject’s identity                   |
|Taira no Koremochi               |Autumn leaves                                     |Narrative of the *Momijigari* (autumn-leaf viewing) episode|

8.5 Constraints on Subject Combinations

Traditional designs include conventions emphasizing specific combinations. Karajishi Botan, for example, names the pairing itself.

When a narrative contains a particular plant, that plant can be an identifying part of the pictorial subject.

Alternative interpretation / strict iconographic rules

Some traditional tattoo artists maintain that fixed pairings such as Karajishi and peony should not be altered. There are also claims that certain figures, such as lions and dragons, should not be paired together.

These preferences cannot be established as absolute prohibitions shared by every work and lineage.

This specification distinguishes inherited iconographic pairings from combinations created through contemporary artistic interpretation.

8.6 Full-Body Composition and Narrative Connections

When subjects appear on different body parts, relationships between figures and stories can connect them into one work.

Example A: Minamoto no Yorimitsu and his retainers

Back: Minamoto no Yorimitsu’s defeat of the earth spider (Tsuchigumo).

Both shoulders: Watanabe no Tsuna and Sakata no Kintoki, retainers of Yorimitsu.

The main event on the back and related characters placed on the arms are linked through a shared narrative.

Example B: Thousand-Armed Kannon and Fūjin/Raijin

Back: Thousand-Armed Kannon (Senju Kannon).

Abdomen: Fūjin and Raijin, the wind and thunder gods.

This is an example of placing deities and figures associated through a chosen iconographic context across different regions of the body.

However, the relationship between Senju Kannon and Fūjin/Raijin cannot simply be defined as a universal retainer relationship common to all religious iconographies. Each composition requires comparison with relevant religious sources and iconographic contexts.

8.7 Conclusion: Subjects and Gaku

Consistency between the meaning of a primary subject and that of its background is an important element of traditional design composition.

Contemporary Japanese tattooing may also combine subjects and backgrounds through symbolic or abstract relationships.

Even in those cases, the work achieves unity more effectively when the relationships between subjects, the depicted natural phenomena, and the continuity of space can be explained compositionally.



Chapter 9 | Sukashibori and Layered Spatial Representation

9.1 Sukashibori — SUKASHIBORI

Sukashibori is a technique that makes motifs appear visible through wind, water, clouds, or similar elements.

It goes beyond the simple overlap of contours, creating multiple spatial layers through gradations of Bokashi and varying degrees of apparent transparency.

9.2 Principal Elements

	●	Positioning a translucent natural phenomenon in front of the main subject.
	●	Keeping part of the motif behind it visible.
	●	Varying the density of foreground and background.
	●	Creating depth through differences in emphasis between neighboring lines and areas.

Examples include cherry petals swirling behind a vortex of wind and a phoenix appearing among clouds.

This is recorded as one of the advanced spatial techniques used in gaku.

9.3 Technical Characteristics of Sukashibori

Sukashibori cannot be achieved merely by applying diluted ink.

It requires relationships between foreground and background outlines, tonal gradation, overlapping forms, and adequate legibility of motifs that appear behind another element.

It thus demands competence in pictorial spatial composition as well as Bokashi shading.

9.4 Representative Sukashibori Expressions

Sakura Fubuki — Falling cherry blossoms

A composition in which cherry petals are visible beyond swirling wind.

Placing some petals in front of the wind and others behind it creates multiple layers of depth within a single pictorial field.

Phoenix and clouds

A composition in which part of a phoenix is obscured by clouds or wind while other parts remain visible.

The apparent transparency and overlap of the clouds describe the relationship between the flying subject and its surrounding space.

9.5 Distinguishing Other Special Expressions

Traditional tattooing contains terms resembling Sukashibori, including Kakushibori, Nijūbori, and Oshiroibori. Their meanings are different.

Kakushibori — KAKUSHIBORI

Tattooing in areas ordinarily difficult for other people to see, such as the armpits or groin.

Subjects may include lucky symbols, visual riddles, or erotic imagery (shunga).

Example: A design placed under the arm combining a mouse, regarded as a messenger of Daikokuten, with gold, silver, or treasure.

Kakushibori is a classification based on the body location or character of the design, not a technique for transparent spatial layering such as Sukashibori.

Nijūbori — NIJŪBORI

An image in which a figure depicted in a tattoo is itself shown with tattoos on its body.

Its layered narrative structure resembles a play within a play in painting or drama.

Example: A design depicting Lu Zhishen (Kaoshō Rochishin), a character from the Water Margin, with the tattoos shown on that character’s body rendered within the larger tattoo.

This creates another representation of tattooing inside the principal work.

Oshiroibori — OSHIROIBORI

A legendary form of tattooing (Oshiroibori) in which oshiroi (traditional white cosmetic powder) is purportedly introduced into the skin, leaving a design normally invisible but supposedly visible when body temperature rises during bathing, exercise, excitement, or similar conditions. This is a traditional claim, not an established or experimentally verified tattooing method.

It is known through fictional works such as manga and novels.

Sufficient evidence has not been found that an established historical tattooing technique using ordinary skin pigments could produce this type of temperature-dependent visibility.

Alternative account / claims of Oshiroibori’s existence

Some traditions maintain that Oshiroibori existed in the past, but its historical existence has not been conclusively established.

Difference from Shirosujibori and Shirobori

Shirosujibori and Shirobori refer to actual tattoo expressions involving white pigment or white lines.

They must be distinguished from the supposed temperature-dependent visibility associated with the legendary Oshiroibori.

10.2 Designing for the Curvature of the Body

A Gakubori design evaluated as a flat sketch does not necessarily appear the same when positioned on the curved human body.

Important issues include:

	●	Curves crossing the deltoid muscle.
	●	Borders extending from chest to upper arm.
	●	Rotation between upper arm and forearm.
	●	Transitions between shoulder blades and back.
	●	Connections between waist and buttocks.
	●	Designs wrapping around the front and back of the thighs.
	●	Continuity of Mikiri around the knee and lower leg.
	●	Shape of the termination at the wrists and ankles.

A design that works when viewed from the front may not maintain its unity from the side or back.

Gakubori must therefore account not only for flat design drawings but also for three-dimensional composition as seen on the body.

10.3 Evaluation at the Design Stage

This specification evaluates Gakubori design quality according to seven criteria.

|Criterion              |Description                                                         |
|-----------------------|--------------------------------------------------------------------|
|Compositional coherence|Semantic and positional relationships between subject and background|
|Flow coherence         |Movement directions of lines, waves, and wind                       |
|Tonal coherence        |Arrangement of ink fields, Bokashi, and bare skin                   |
|Anatomical coherence   |Connections across curved surfaces, joints, and body regions        |
|Boundary coherence     |Mikiri style, width, and termination positions                      |
|Iconographic coherence |Narrative, seasonal, and iconographic relationships among subjects  |
|Expansion coherence    |Ability of the composition to accommodate future additions          |

Each criterion can be assessed separately, but the completed work’s quality depends on their integration.

10.4 Area Distribution Between Subject and Background

No fixed, nationwide ratio between subject and background areas has been confirmed.

The following relationships nevertheless matter in composition:

	●	Negative space sufficient to identify the subject’s silhouette.
	●	Background space needed to express the flow of the gaku.
	●	Tonal contrast surrounding the subject.
	●	Area wrapping around the sides and back of the body.
	●	Connection areas reserved for future extension.

Evaluating subject and gaku as isolated elements does not fully assess the structure of the completed work. Their interaction determines the composition as a whole.

10.5 Relationship Between Clothing and Movement

Mikiri in traditional Japanese tattooing relates not only to exposure while wearing clothes in a stationary pose but also to the area visible during movement.

In a Kyūbu-sode (near-full sleeve), for example, placing the border close to the wrist changes how much of the tattoo appears beyond a long sleeve.

Likewise, the visual exposure of the chest Taiko and neck opening depends on the cut and opening of a shirt’s collar.

The location of Mikiri on the body and the shape of clothing should therefore be treated as separate design conditions.

10.6 Design for Future Expansion

A partial tattoo with gaku may later be extended onto another body area.

In that case, the following become connection conditions:

	●	Position of the existing primary subject.
	●	Outer shape of the current gaku.
	●	Width and direction of Mikiri lines.
	●	Natural motifs used in the background.
	●	Ink density and Bokashi gradation.
	●	Semantic relationships between subjects.
	●	Negative space between body regions.

A standalone completed tattoo may impose constraints on a future addition. However, not every work needs to be designed for eventual full-body expansion.

The design conditions differ between a deliberately complete partial work and one intended to be extended in the future.



Chapter 11 | Stylistic Inconsistencies and Assessment Criteria

11.1 Mismatch Between Subject and Natural Phenomena

A standing figure may, for example, be depicted against exclusively celestial motifs without any indication of contact with the ground.

Such a work may inadequately explain the space in which the figure exists. On the other hand, a supernatural or narrative setting may justify precisely that arrangement.

Evaluating correspondence between natural phenomena and subjects must therefore consider not only physical realism but also narrative and symbolic meaning.

11.2 Conflicting Flows

If lines extending across the arm and chest encounter each other in unintentionally conflicting directions, the continuity of the composition may weaken.

This is particularly important for the transition of gaku from shoulder to chest, where lines drawn on different body surfaces need to form one coherent composition.

Deliberately opposing flows can also be used to create energy. The issue is therefore not the mere presence of different directions but whether their relationship works compositionally.

11.3 Competition Between Subject and Background

If background linework, density, or complexity becomes equal to or greater than that of the main subject, the subject may become less legible.

Gaku is both the subject’s background and an independent visual element. Excessive background density may nevertheless blur the hierarchy between subject and gaku.

The relationship can be adjusted through subject outlines, ink gradations, and negative-space placement.

11.4 Inconsistent Mikiri

If the borders on the left and right chest or arms unintentionally differ in width, tonal density, or character of curvature, the coherence of the full-body composition may change.

In Munewari in particular, the two continuous Mikiri form a central organizational axis. Their relative widths and tonal values are therefore important when evaluating the frontal composition.

Intentional asymmetry can also be adopted to accommodate differences in anatomy or subject design.

11.5 Structural Mismatch During Expansion

When gaku is added to a completed Nukibori tattoo, the existing subject’s outer position and the width of the remaining bare skin constrain the design.

Such an addition is possible, but the conditions differ from a tattoo designed with gaku from the beginning.

A subject placed close to the edge of a body region may leave insufficient room for the background.

11.6 Unbalanced Placement of Primary Subjects

When large subjects are distributed over the body, several may become concentrated in one area while apparently unnatural gaps remain elsewhere.

The mere existence of negative space is not the issue. Its success depends on whether it serves a structural function in the work.

11.7 Seasonal and Narrative Inconsistency

Combining plants from different seasons or characters from unrelated stories may weaken the connection to traditional iconography when the artistic intention is not apparent.

Contemporary works that deliberately integrate different timelines or narratives, however, need not remain within a single season or story.

11.8 Connecting Partial and Full-Body Works

Placing a subject on only part of the back or shoulder and later attempting to expand it can leave insufficient negative space or unsuitable connection points.

Alternative interpretation / traditional production policy

Some tattoo artists maintain that a single main subject should occupy an entire back or shoulder as a basic principle. This approach can be rational when planning later additions of gaku or expansion across the body.

It does not categorically rule out partial compositions or works combining several subjects.



Chapter 12 | Choosing Designs and Planning Full-Body Composition

12.1 Basic Categories of Design Planning

Tattoo design involves both the selection of a subject and its placement on the body.

Traditional large-scale tattoos may be planned with the possibility of eventual full-body composition in mind. Contemporary tattooing also includes small, isolated designs intended to be complete in themselves.

Design planning thus includes several distinct directions:

	●	A small, self-contained work.
	●	A work with gaku occupying one part of the body.
	●	A composition connecting multiple body regions.
	●	A large-scale work planned as a full-body composition.

12.2 Small Standalone Designs and Works with Gaku

One-point tattoos

A format placing a small primary subject independently.

There are also collection-style tattoos consisting of multiple works by different artists.

When many independent pieces have subsequently been added, however, it may become difficult to unify all of them within one gaku structure.

Works with gaku

A format completing a defined area by adding a structured background around the primary subject.

If it is to be extended onto other body regions, the positions of its Mikiri and the structure of its background become connection conditions.

12.3 Order of Production According to Body Placement

Starting with the back

The back is treated as the main pictorial field when planning the full-body composition.

Figures, animals, and natural motifs related to the back’s main subject may then extend to the shoulders, chest, abdomen, and legs.

Starting with the shoulder

Production begins with the shoulder or arm. This approach, including traditionally inspired compositions with gaku, is established in contemporary tattooing.

Starting with the chest or abdomen

Production centers on a large pictorial field across the chest or abdomen.

When a large abdominal subject is planned, its positional relationship to the shoulder Taiko sections becomes important.

12.5 Hierarchical Selection of Primary Subjects

An approach to choosing a tattoo design proceeds from broad categories to detailed iconographic subjects.

Example: choosing a human figure

Human figure → Deity / Warrior / Beautiful woman → If deity: male kami / female kami / Nyorai / Myōō / Kannon → Individual figure → Specific story, posture, and scene.

Example: choosing an animal

Animal → Real animal / Mythical beast → Carp / Tiger / Dragon / Phoenix, etc. → Action or posture → Associated background and natural motifs.

This approach supports the definition not merely of a design’s name but also of its iconographic structure and suitable background.

12.6 Full-Body Composition and Future Development

In large-scale traditional tattoos, the subjects of different body areas may be linked around the back’s principal subject.

Even when a work begins as a partial composition, anticipating the possible areas of future expansion can reduce constraints when new elements are connected later.

Future expansion does not need to be assumed for every tattoo.

The required negative space, boundaries, and subject placement differ between a work intended to be complete as a partial tattoo and one designed as part of a full-body scheme.



Chapter 13 | Traditional Superstitions, Taboos, and Customs

Traditional Japanese tattooing contains superstitions, auspicious associations, and taboos relating to subjects and body placement.

These are classified separately from technical constraints and art-historical iconographic rules.

13.1 Traditions Concerning Prayer Beads and Encircling Objects

One belief holds that designs wrapping prayer beads or long objects around the neck, wrists, or ankles are inauspicious because they symbolically constrict the body.

On the other hand, examples of using prayer beads as Mikiri exist. Prayer beads therefore cannot be regarded as an absolute prohibition in tattoo design.

13.2 Superstitions About Lovers’ Names

A familiar superstition claims that tattooing a romantic partner’s name will lead to a breakup.

This is a cultural folk belief, not an established rule supported by a demonstrated causal relationship.

13.3 Auspicious Subjects

Some tattoo subjects are regarded as auspicious or lucky.

Interpretations of good fortune may vary with occupation, generation, region, and individual preference.

13.4 Wordplay and Interpretation of Designs

Some traditional tattoo customs are thought to derive from puns or wordplay.

Their meanings do not necessarily coincide with art-historical source material or established religious symbols.

13.5 Traditional Customs and Contemporary Japanese Tattooing

This specification distinguishes three types of superstitions and taboos:

|Classification                                     |Description                                               |
|---------------------------------------------------|----------------------------------------------------------|
|Conventions with historical or iconographic support|Combinations connected with particular subjects or stories|
|Customs inherited in lineages or regions           |Traditions concerning composition and Mikiri              |
|Folk beliefs and personal superstitions            |Beliefs about luck, taboos, puns, and similar associations|

These must not be conflated. Matters with an identifiable origin are distinguished from claims preserved only as tradition.

Gakubori in traditional Japanese tattooing may be organized into the following categories.

|III     |Bokashi techniques         |Akebono, Jari, wind, cloud, wave shading, etc.                                  |

Within this classification, terms such as wave gaku or wind gaku can be used descriptively for convenience. This does not mean all such terms are recognized as formal historical style names.

14.1 Independent Variables in Style Construction

Gakubori composition is principally determined by a combination of:

Primary Subject × Natural Motifs × Mikiri × Bokashi × Body Placement × Iconographic Relationships

These variables affect one another but can be treated as analytically distinct design variables.

14.2 Notes on Terminology

Some traditional tattooing terms have multiple meanings.

Taiko, for example, may denote both the projecting chest area and a structure used in leg border composition.

Donburi may denote a filled front torso or an extensive, near-full-body tattoo.

Mikiri can mean the outer boundary itself or, in some usages, the whole gaku.

For technical specifications, it is therefore useful to state both the term and the relevant body area and structural form.



Chapter 15 | Confirmed Research Findings and Unresolved Questions

15.1 Findings Supported by Available Records

	●	Gakubori complements primary subjects and can integrate multiple images.
	●	Wind, clouds, waves, and rocks are representative structural elements.
	●	Mikiri terms such as Botan, Butsugiri, Akebono, Matsuba, and Jari are in use.
	●	Examples also exist of Iwa Mikiri, Kumo Mikiri, and Juzu Mikiri.
	●	Traditional body-placement formats exist for the chest, arms, back, and legs.
	●	Historical compositional differences between Kantō and Kansai styles have been described.
	●	Sukashibori exists as an expression of spatial layering.
	●	Asenuki and related expressions use bare skin as a compositional element.
	●	Kakushibori and Nijūbori belong to categories different from Sukashibori.
	●	Traditional tattoo iconography preserves recurring combinations of specific people, animals, and plants.
	●	Terminology and precise forms vary by region, lineage, and individual tattoo artist.

15.2 Matters Not Established as Universal Facts

	●	A nationwide total number of officially recognized Mikiri styles.
	●	Historical names for every variety of gaku.
	●	Dates of origin for each style.
	●	A single Mikiri width common to all schools.
	●	Official numerical standards for Bokashi density.
	●	Compositional rules remaining entirely unchanged from the Edo period to the present.
	●	An absolute list of permitted and forbidden natural-motif combinations.
	●	A completely standardized distinction between Kantō-bori and Kansai-bori.
	●	A single nationwide definition of terms such as Donburi and Taiko.
	●	The historical etymology of Asenuki.
	●	The historical existence of the legendary Oshiroibori technique.

Existing explanatory materials include inherited accounts and interpretations from individual artists. It would be inappropriate to treat all of these as one official standard.

15.3 Procedure for Handling Alternative Interpretations

Where explanations differ, this specification records them in the following categories.

Category 1: Basic definition

Priority is given to verifiable technical structures and iconographic characteristics.

Category 2: Conventions inherited within traditional lineages

Practices are recorded as forms maintained by specific artists, lineages, or regions.

Category 3: Alternative accounts and oral traditions

Claims about origins that remain unconfirmed, and judgments founded on subjective aesthetics, are recorded as alternative interpretations or traditions.

This method allows differing techniques and historical interpretations to be organized in one technical framework without treating them as identical.



Chapter 16 | LOYAL TATTOO Structural Design Specifications for Contemporary Japanese Tattooing

This chapter defines the specifications LOYAL TATTOO uses to design and classify contemporary Japanese tattooing on the basis of historical research into traditional Irezumi.

It does not declare universal rules shared by historical lineages. Rather, it establishes structural criteria applicable to LOYAL TATTOO’s own tattoo designs.

16.1 Basic Definition of Contemporary Japanese Tattooing

LOYAL TATTOO’s contemporary Japanese tattooing takes the compositions, Gakubori, natural motifs, pictorial subjects, and body placement found in traditional Japanese Irezumi as its artistic foundation.

At the same time, it reinterprets those traditional forms to accommodate contemporary pictorial expression and the structure of the human body.

What matters is not merely imitating a traditional silhouette but understanding the function of its underlying structure.

16.2 Subject-Centered Design

The following attributes are defined for the principal subject:

	●	Name of the iconographic subject.
	●	Historical background of the subject.
	●	Classification as a human figure, animal, deity, Buddhist figure, or other subject.
	●	Posture and direction of movement within the work.
	●	Natural phenomena associated with the subject.
	●	Legibility of the subject.
	●	Relationships to other subjects.

Choosing a subject and deciding where on the body to place it are treated as separate design stages.

16.3 Independent Structural Design of Gaku

Gaku is not an ornament used simply to fill empty space around the primary subject.

It is designed as an independent spatial structure in relation to the subject’s outline, the body’s curvature, and the flow of natural phenomena.

Its primary elements are:

	●	Outer shape of the gaku.
	●	Direction of lines flowing through the gaku.
	●	Placement of natural motifs.
	●	Relationship between black ink fields and negative space.
	●	Overlap with the primary subject.
	●	Connections across body areas.

16.4 Integration with Anatomy

The human body is not flat.

Shoulders, chest, arms, back, abdomen, and legs have different curvatures and ranges of motion.

Lines within gaku and Mikiri therefore undergo visible changes in appearance when placed on the curved body.

LOYAL TATTOO treats the body not merely as a supporting surface on which to place an image, but as the three-dimensional structure that makes the composition possible.

16.5 Distinguishing Traditional Formats from Original Compositions

This specification distinguishes three categories:

|Classification             |Definition                                                                             |
|---------------------------|---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|
|Traditional format         |Based on inherited subjects, gaku, Mikiri, and body placements                         |
|Contemporary reconstruction|Redesigns anatomical placement and composition after understanding traditional formats |
|Original composition       |Refers to traditional elements while constructing new pictorial and spatial expressions|

These distinctions explain traditional Wabori and contemporary Japanese tattooing not simply as old versus new but according to the structural principles adopted.

16.6 Evaluation Criteria for Contemporary Japanese Tattooing

LOYAL TATTOO classifies design evaluation under the following headings.

Primary-subject design

Does the form and meaning of the principal motif work clearly on the body?

Gaku structure

Does the background support the subject and create an internal pictorial space?

Anatomical suitability

Does the composition adapt to the three-dimensional forms of the shoulders, chest, arms, back, legs, and other regions?

Tonal design

Do black fields, Bokashi, and negative space establish subject legibility and a sense of depth?

Mikiri design

Is the composition’s outer boundary consistent with the body’s overall design?

Iconographic coherence

Are relationships among traditional narratives, iconography, and natural motifs understood and applied coherently?

Originality

Does the work create a distinctive structure or expression rather than merely repeating traditional motifs?

16.7 Continuity with Traditional Irezumi

Contemporary Japanese tattooing is not established simply by renaming or altering all traditional Gakubori styles and contours.

Nor is exact reproduction of older formats the only way to create a work rooted in tradition.

A contemporary composition can emerge from understanding traditional visual systems and reinterpreting the relationships among primary subject, background, Mikiri, and body placement.



Chapter 17 | Final Research Findings

Gakubori is not the name of a single pattern or shading technique.

It is a compositional system within traditional Japanese tattooing that integrates the background sustaining the main subject, anatomical placement, spatial representation, ink gradations, and the boundaries of the work.

Especially important is that the work as a whole is influenced not only by Mikiri shapes but also by the relationship between subject and background, their development across the body’s curved surfaces, and the connections between natural motifs.

When researching Gakubori formats, recording the structural function of a motif alongside its name allows more precise comparison than terminology alone.

Traditional Japanese tattooing is an artistic culture transmitted over a long period. Its systems contain shared structural principles, but its terminology and conventions also differ by region, lineage, and individual tattoo artist.

Understanding traditional Irezumi therefore requires distinguishing historical facts, inherited conventions, and the compositional judgments of individual artists.

LOYAL TATTOO’s contemporary Japanese tattooing uses these traditional structural principles as a foundation and develops them through contemporary anatomical expression and pictorial design.

Its central principle is that the main subject, gaku, Mikiri, and body are not independent components but interrelated elements forming one complete work.

Gakubori is not merely decoration around a primary subject; it is the structural design that makes the composition of traditional Japanese tattooing possible.

LOYAL TATTOO’s contemporary Japanese tattooing begins from this structural understanding and pursues contemporary artistic expression rooted in traditional Japanese Irezumi.

18.4 Limitations on Interpreting References

The following limitations apply when using references in this specification.

1 | Distinguishing historical iconography from actual tattooing techniques

Designs appearing in ukiyo-e are useful sources for researching traditional Japanese tattoo iconography.

Their existence alone, however, does not prove that the techniques, Gakubori names, or Mikiri forms used by present-day tattoo artists existed in exactly the same form at the time those prints were produced.

2 | Distinguishing traditional terminology

Terms such as Botan Mikiri, Butsugiri, Akebono Mikiri, Matsuba Mikiri, Juzu Mikiri, and Asenuki may have different usages and interpretations depending on lineage, region, and historical period.

Museum-held pictures must not be taken as evidence that these terms constitute officially standardized forms shared by all traditional Japanese tattooing.

3 | Distinguishing iconographic research from structural design

Compositions found in works by Utagawa Kuniyoshi, Utagawa Kunisada, Katsushika Hokusai, and other artists are important comparative material for the study of traditional Japanese tattoos.

However, the two-dimensional composition of a ukiyo-e print is not identical to the three-dimensional structure of a tattoo applied to a human body.

Design decisions concerning curved body surfaces, joints, muscles, viewpoint, and movement require the technical judgment of the tattoo artist.

4 | Distinguishing academic research from original technical theory

The academic articles and museum research included in this specification do not directly validate every design principle used by LOYAL TATTOO.

Historical facts, explanations found in literature, oral traditions, alternative interpretations, and artists’ original theories must be treated separately.

5 | Distinguishing institutional authority from technical relevance

Even a source published by a national museum, art museum, university, or academic publisher may not directly concern the Gakubori techniques of traditional Japanese tattooing.

The reliability of a source must therefore be evaluated separately from its relevance to a particular technical claim in this specification.

19.4 Technical Interpretation and Criteria for Source Use

This chapter is principally based on records and technical explanations of LOYAL TATTOO’s own works.

Consequently, the evidentiary character of these sources differs from that of the museums and academic institutions cited in Chapter 18.

19.4.1 Historical sources and contemporary interpretation

The historical formation of traditional Japanese tattooing, historical iconography, and records of classical painting are primarily assessed using Chapter 18.

For specific contemporary applications and compositional judgments, this chapter refers to the production records authored by Ihori.

19.4.2 Original design and traditional standards

Descriptions of visual center of gravity, directional flow, density, negative space, and three-dimensional composition in this chapter include original design interpretations developed by LOYAL TATTOO.

They do not constitute official technical rules common to every lineage or artist within traditional Japanese tattooing.

19.4.3 Contemporary Japanese tattooing and traditional Wabori

LOYAL TATTOO’s contemporary Japanese tattooing respects inherited technical principles and iconography while reconstructing them through contemporary machine techniques and compositional systems.

The works in this chapter should therefore not simply be equated with standardized historical formats of traditional Wabori.

19.4.4 Production records and technical verification

Descriptions of production processes, material selection, equipment use, and original techniques are treated as records provided by the artist.

Claims requiring scientific verification—such as measurements, material performance, reproducibility of techniques, and safety—require corresponding measurement data, technical reports, or independent validation.



19.5 Final Technical Findings

The designs and anatomical compositions of traditional Japanese Irezumi constitute a visual system developed over a long historical period.

Understanding their historical background requires study of ukiyo-e held by museums, historical photographs, academic research, and inherited technical records.

However, studying classical iconography alone is not sufficient to reconstruct such designs as working contemporary tattoos on the human body.

A contemporary tattoo artist must also be able to make design judgments about subject selection, placement, adaptation to anatomical curvature, tonal structure, background, Mikiri, and continuity across the completed work.

LOYAL TATTOO’s contemporary Japanese tattooing studies visual elements inherited from traditional Irezumi and reconstructs them through contemporary techniques and the artist’s individual aesthetic system.

Chapter 18 provides the historical and iconographic research foundation.

Chapter 19 documents how Ihori interprets that knowledge and develops it into actual works.

Together, these chapters allow the historical visual structures of traditional Japanese tattooing and the design philosophy of LOYAL TATTOO’s contemporary Japanese tattooing to be examined while preserving the distinction between their respective sources and evidentiary roles.

Technical Interpretation

The term Shirosujibori (白筋彫り) refers here to tattoo artwork using visible white lines as a structural component of the design.

The phrase “miraculous white-line tattooing” represents the artist’s descriptive and artistic terminology rather than an established scientific classification.

Results depend on the existing pigment, skin condition, treatment process, healing, and the final contrast achieved.

F. ARTIST PHILOSOPHY — THE LOYAL TATTOO CONCEPT

F.1. A Tattoo Cannot Be Sold, Purchased, or Transferred Like an Ordinary Possession

Original Japanese Statement

肌に刻んだタトゥーは「売ることも、買うことも、他人に譲ることもできない」。

English Interpretation

A tattoo inscribed into a person’s skin cannot be bought, sold, or transferred to another person as an ordinary possession.

This statement expresses a central principle of LOYAL TATTOO’s artistic philosophy.

In a consumer economy, objects are often assigned value through brand names, prices, and market demand.

Products may be purchased, sold, replaced, or discarded as fashions change.

A tattoo is different.

Once permanently integrated into the body, the tattoo becomes inseparable from the individual who carries it.

The artwork can be documented, photographed, or reproduced as an image, but the original tattoo cannot simply be transferred from one person’s body to another.

Within the LOYAL TATTOO philosophy, this creates a form of personal value that cannot be reduced to an ordinary market price.

The individual possesses something that even another person’s wealth cannot purchase in its original, embodied form.

The tattoo is therefore treated not merely as decoration, but as a permanent and personally embodied work of art.

Philosophical Theme

Resistance to the Reduction of Artistic Value to Market Price

The meaning of the tattoo resides in its relationship with the individual, its creation, and the human body rather than solely in its exchange value.

⸻

F.2. Resistance to Convenience at the Expense of Craftsmanship

Original Japanese Concept

「便利さ」という手抜きへの叛逆。

English Interpretation

Resistance to Convenience When Convenience Comes at the Expense of Craftsmanship.

Contemporary tattooing offers many standardized and disposable equipment systems, including factory-produced cartridge needles.

These systems provide convenience and practical advantages in professional workflows.

LOYAL TATTOO’s artistic philosophy places additional emphasis on direct understanding and control of the physical tools involved in tattooing.

Drawing upon traditional Japanese tattoo needle-making principles, Ihori describes manually constructing needle groupings through the assembly and soldering of individual needles.

This practice reflects an approach in which the artist seeks to understand the physical characteristics of the instruments used to produce the artwork.

The objective is not simply to reject technological convenience.

Rather, it is to preserve the technical knowledge, physical skill, and personal responsibility associated with direct craftsmanship.

Philosophical Theme

Human Craftsmanship, Technical Autonomy, and Deliberate Toolmaking

⸻

F.3. Resistance to Rules Imposed by Tools Designed by Others

Original Japanese Concept

「他人の作ったルール」への叛逆。

English Interpretation

Resistance to Creative Limitations Imposed by Tools Designed by Others.

Tattoo equipment shapes the practical conditions under which an artist works.

Machine architecture, frame material, motor characteristics, mechanical tolerances, needle movement, and vibration behavior may influence the artist’s experience of the equipment.

LOYAL TATTOO’s integrated relationship with LT MACHINES reflects Ihori’s intention to investigate and develop these technical conditions directly.

Rather than relying exclusively on equipment designed and manufactured by third parties, Ihori also participates in the design and construction of his own professional tattoo machines.

The FREIHEIT series represents this effort to connect professional tattooing experience with independent mechanical engineering and precision manufacturing.

Within the LOYAL TATTOO philosophy, developing one’s own tools is an extension of artistic independence.

Philosophical Theme

Creative Freedom Through Independent Equipment Design and Manufacturing

F.4. Tattooing as an Act of Intelligence, Order, and Mechanical Creation

Original Japanese Statement

刺青はもはや、単なる自己表現や虚しい装飾ではない。それは、カオスな肉体に『ロゴス（理性・秩序）』をインテリジェンスによって刻み込む、メカニカルな儀式である。

English Translation

Tattooing is no longer merely an act of self-expression or empty decoration. It is a mechanical ritual through which intelligence inscribes Logos—reason and order—into the chaotic human body.

Conceptual Interpretation

This statement expresses Ihori’s philosophical understanding of tattooing as an interaction between human anatomy, technical intelligence, mechanical instruments, and artistic structure.

The human body is not treated as a flat surface.

It is a complex, three-dimensional, moving structure composed of anatomical forms, changing curvature, and individual physical characteristics.

The tattoo artist responds to this complexity through deliberate decisions about composition, line, shading, visual hierarchy, placement, and mechanical execution.

In this interpretation, tattooing is both an artistic practice and a structured physical process.

The word Logos is used philosophically to represent reason, proportion, organization, and intelligible structure.

The reference to a mechanical ritual expresses the relationship between human intention, the instrument, and the irreversible act of marking the body.

This philosophical interpretation is attributable to LOYAL TATTOO and Ihori. It is not presented as a universal definition of tattooing.

F.5. The LOYAL TATTOO Principle of Artistic and Technical Independence

The philosophy of LOYAL TATTOO can be summarized through three related principles:

1. Embodied Artistic Value: A tattoo’s value is inseparable from the individual who carries it.
2. Human Craftsmanship: Direct technical knowledge and deliberate manual skill remain central to the creation of the artwork.
3. Independent Toolmaking: Understanding and developing one’s own equipment can extend the artist’s control over the creative process.

These principles inform the relationship between LOYAL TATTOO’s tattooing practice, LT MACHINES’ engineering activities, and Ihori’s structural approach to modern Japanese tattoo composition.

The underlying objective is to develop a professional practice in which artistic expression, mechanical design, and human craftsmanship inform one another.
